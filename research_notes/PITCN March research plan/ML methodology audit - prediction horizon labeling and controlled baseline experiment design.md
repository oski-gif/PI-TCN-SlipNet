# ML methodology audit: prediction horizon labeling and controlled baseline experiment design for imbalanced time-series slip prediction

## How should a prediction horizon H be chosen and justified for this specific task (grasp-and-pull, ~1900-step trials)?

### Takeaway
Across the literature, H is chosen by two joint constraints — (1) it must be long enough to be *actionable* (matched to whatever downstream control/response latency the prediction feeds, when one exists) and (2) it must be long enough to keep the positive class from vanishing — and it is validated empirically by sweeping a small set of candidate H values and reporting how metrics change, not by picking one value a priori. No source gave a closed-form rule for picking H in a slip-prediction task specifically; the strongest direct precedent (Veiga et al.'s BioTac slip-prediction horizon study) could not be read in full.

### Cited Findings
- In a clinical early-warning benchmark, horizons were chosen per failure type based on clinical actionability, not a single fixed value: 8 hours for circulatory failure, 24 hours for decompensation, 48 hours for kidney failure — [Towards Foundation Models for Critical Care Time Series, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.16346)
- A vineyard-disease early-warning study directly tested horizon sensitivity, comparing 1–3, 3–7, and 5–10 day windows; the 3–7 day window gave the best F1 across all four model families tested, and narrower horizons produced fewer positive training examples, which hurt detection — [Event-Based Early Warning of Vineyard Disease Risk, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.04548)
- Veiga, Peters & Hermans (IEEE Transactions on Haptics, 2018) explicitly frame slip as a supervised-learning problem that predicts the *future* occurrence of slip from BioTac tactile data, and they explicitly study how the prediction time horizon affects accuracy, alongside which input features and tactile channels matter — [Veiga et al. ToH 2018 (PDF)](https://www.ias.informatik.tu-darmstadt.de/uploads/Publications/veigaToH2018.pdf)
- The stated motivation for predicting slip ahead of time (rather than just detecting it) in this group's earlier work is to compensate for controller/actuation latency — i.e., the prediction needs enough lead time for a grip-force controller to actually act before slip occurs — [IROS 2015 Veiga et al. (PDF)](https://www.ias.tu-darmstadt.de/uploads/Site/EditPublication/IROS2015veiga.pdf); a secondary summary of the 2018 paper notes BioTac was sampled at 100 Hz and F-score was the evaluation metric used to compare horizons — [lab slide deck summarizing Veiga et al.](https://walt.web.illinois.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/Qual_Presentation_BTW_5_27_22_online.pdf)
- In general feedback-control literature, prediction/look-ahead horizon is explicitly set to match total system latency (sensing delay + actuation delay), so the controller can act on a state estimate before it is stale — one patent sets the horizon to the sum of sensing delay and the maximum expected actuation latency — [wireless feedback control patent](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11551058); a vehicle drift-control study found that oscillations became severe once actuator delay exceeded roughly 95 ms, and fixed this by setting a state-predictor horizon to approximately the mean actuator latency — [Drifting in the Future, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.27914)
- "Early classification of time series" (ECTS) is studied as its own ML subfield that explicitly trades earliness against accuracy; a 2025 TMLR survey/benchmark of nine state-of-the-art ECTS methods explicitly notes there is no shared evaluation protocol across the field for comparing earliness/horizon choices — [Early Classification of Time Series: A Survey and Benchmark, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.18332); the TEASER method, a widely cited ECTS baseline, uses a two-tier design (one classifier that predicts as data arrives, a second that decides whether the prediction is reliable enough to emit yet) and was reported 2–3x earlier than competitors at similar accuracy across 45 benchmark datasets — [arXiv 1908.03405](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.03405)

### Inferences
- The project's own diagnostic finding (pull_force rises continuously and smoothly across the whole ~180-timestep window before slip, with no abrupt local tactile signature) suggests slip here is a gradually-approaching threshold-crossing event rather than a sudden-onset anomaly. That shape argues for treating H as a tunable design axis to sweep empirically (as the vineyard study did with 1–3/3–7/5–10-day windows) rather than picking a single "correct" H from first principles — a natural first sweep, given the already-visualized 180-timestep event window, is something like H ∈ {10, 30, 60, 100, 150} timesteps, reporting F1/PR-AUC/balanced accuracy at each.
- Because this project doesn't yet have a concrete downstream controller with a known reaction-time budget (that's "idea 2/3" for later), there is currently no hard control-latency floor to anchor H to, unlike the Veiga/IROS and MPC-style precedents where H is pinned to a known actuator/sensing delay. Until a specific closed-loop response time is defined, H should be chosen primarily by the empirical sweep above, with the explicit caveat that it may need revisiting once a real controller latency is known.
- The vineyard study's finding that narrower horizons worsen class imbalance is a useful early warning: shrinking H will not just change "how early" the model predicts, it will also shrink the number of positive frames, compounding the existing imbalance problem. Any H sweep should report the resulting positive-class prevalence at each H value, not just performance metrics.

### Gaps
- Could not extract the actual horizon values Veiga et al. tested or the quantitative horizon-vs-accuracy curve from the ToH 2018 paper — the PDF's text is embedded in compressed streams that the fetch tool could not decode, and no secondary source reported the specific numbers. This is the single most directly relevant paper to this question and is worth the student manually opening the PDF (link above) to read the horizon-ablation table/figure directly.
- No source was found that ties a specific robot-hand/gripper actuation or control-loop latency number to a recommended minimum H; the control-theory precedent (horizon ≥ sensing + actuation delay) is generic and not slip-prediction-specific, and this project's own MuJoCo closed-loop latency characteristics weren't available to me as a researcher to connect to it.
- No literature was found specifying whether H should be expressed/tuned in simulation timesteps or physical time (seconds); given the project's trials run ~1900 timesteps, I'd flag reporting H in both units (timesteps and sim-dt × H seconds) as a reasonable practice, but this is my own inference, not something a cited source recommends.

## What's a correct way to relabel y_series for a horizon-based target, and what edge cases (trial-level train/val/test leakage, ambiguous boundary frames) need handling?

### Takeaway
The standard pattern for horizon labeling is: label timestep t positive if the event occurs at ANY point within [t, t+H], negative only if the trial is confirmed stable across the ENTIRE window [t, t+H], and drop timesteps that can't be unambiguously classified under that rule rather than forcing a guess; separately, leakage must be prevented at both the trial level (whole trials never split across train/val/test) and the label-horizon level (training rows whose horizon window overlaps a held-out window must be purged).

### Cited Findings
- In the clinical early-warning benchmark's labeling scheme: a sample gets a positive label if any future time point within the horizon shows the failure state; it gets a negative label only when there's confirmed stability within the whole horizon; timesteps that are ambiguous under this rule are dropped from training entirely rather than force-labeled — [Towards Foundation Models for Critical Care Time Series, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.16346) (note: I could not re-verify this by reading the paper's PDF text directly — WebFetch could not decode its compressed PDF streams — so this is relayed from the search-engine's summary of the paper rather than a direct quote I verified myself)
- Group-based (not row-based) splitting is the standard fix when samples are not independent (e.g., repeated trials/sessions from the same unit): splitting by row instead of by group lets the model learn unit-specific patterns that don't generalize, and is called out as a documented misuse pattern — [bioLeak package documentation](https://mirror.uned.ac.cr/cran/web/packages/bioLeak/vignettes/bioLeak-intro.html)
- When labels are built from a forward-looking horizon window, standard shuffled k-fold leaks information because overlapping horizons span the train/test boundary; the "purged cross-validation" approach explicitly removes ("purges") training rows whose label horizon overlaps the test window's time range, and adds an "embargo" — dropping rows immediately after the test window too, since they remain serially correlated with it — [purged-cross-validation (GitHub)](https://github.com/eslazarev/purged-cross-validation)
- The correct unit for held-out evaluation in grouped/sequential data is the whole group, not individual rows: a human-robot painting-attribution study holds out an entire painting per fold (not individual patches) specifically to prevent leakage from spatially correlated regions within the same painting — [Patch-Based Spatial Authorship Attribution, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.17030); a robotic-surgery gesture recognition review similarly contrasts "leave-one-supertrial-out" (holding out one trial per user) against leave-one-user-out as the two standard grouped-validation choices in that domain — [Gesture Recognition in Robotic Surgery: a Review, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.00027)
- General time-ordered-split guidance: ordinary shuffled k-fold on time series trains on future data to predict the past; walk-forward/blocked training (train only on earlier blocks, test on a later block) avoids this — [temporalcv docs: Common Pitfalls](https://temporalcv.readthedocs.io/en/latest/guide/common_pitfalls.html)

### Inferences
- Applying the critical-care rule directly to this project's y_series: for each timestep t in a trial, the horizon label should be y_H(t) = 1 if the raw slip label is 1 at any t' in [t, t+H], and y_H(t) = 0 only if the raw label stays 0 for the entire window [t, t+H]. Timesteps within H of the end of the trial (where fewer than H future steps actually exist) are the direct analogue of the "ambiguous" case in the critical-care scheme and should be dropped from training/eval rather than silently truncated or guessed — this is the single most important and easiest-to-miss edge case for a ~1900-step trial with a fixed H.
- Because trials here are single continuous episodes (not independent "patient-moments" as in the clinical analogy), leakage control needs TWO layers that happen to coincide for this project: (a) ordinary trial-level leakage — no trial may have any of its timesteps split across train/val/test, which is the grouped-splitting precedent above — and (b) horizon-overlap leakage, which for this project is automatically satisfied once (a) is enforced, since a trial's horizon windows never extend into a different trial. The purge/embargo concept becomes relevant only if the student ever moves to a scheme where splits happen WITHIN a trial by time (e.g., using the first half of a trial for training and second half for validation); in that within-trial case, rows near the split boundary whose horizon window crosses it must be purged, exactly per the purged-CV precedent.
- A project-specific ambiguity not resolved by any source found: how to label timesteps AFTER slip has already begun. The "any future positive within H" rule, taken literally, would keep labeling post-onset timesteps as positive for H steps after the window stops containing a slip at all — but it says nothing about whether post-onset frames should even be included in a prediction task framed as "early warning of an upcoming failure" (as opposed to "is failure ongoing"). This needs an explicit, documented project decision (e.g., truncate each trial's horizon-labeling exercise at slip onset, or convert the task to stop evaluating after first slip), since the literature uniformly works with tasks that don't revisit this case.

## What do AUROC/precision-recall/balanced-accuracy actually add beyond F1/accuracy here, and how to compute them correctly with trial-level (not frame-level) cross-validation/uncertainty estimates given limited compute?

### Takeaway
PR curves expose precision collapse under class imbalance that ROC curves can hide (two settings with identical ROC points can have precision of 75% vs 25%), but there is a live, unresolved disagreement in the metrics literature about whether ROC-AUC is actually "robust" or "misleadingly stable" under imbalance — so the safe practice is to report both, always alongside the explicit positive-class prevalence; separately, because trials are internally autocorrelated, any uncertainty estimate (CI, seed spread) must be computed by resampling whole TRIALS, not individual frames, or it will be badly overconfident.

### Cited Findings
- Saito & Rehmsmeier (PLOS ONE, 2015) show with a worked numeric example that ROC curves are mathematically insensitive to class-ratio changes (same TPR/FPR → same ROC curve) in a way that hides a real change in false-positive counts: 500 true positives paired with 160 false positives (balanced) vs. the same 500 true positives paired with 1,600 false positives (10x more imbalanced) produce an *identical* ROC point, but precision drops from 75% to 25% — a drop only the PR curve shows — [The Precision-Recall Plot Is More Informative than the ROC Plot, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0118432)
- The PR-curve baseline for a random classifier is NOT 0.5 — it equals the positive-class prevalence P/(P+N) — so PR-AUC values are only meaningful when reported alongside that prevalence number, and should not be compared across datasets/subsets with different positive rates — [same PLOS ONE source](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0118432)
- Saito & Rehmsmeier's own recommendation: "the results of our study strongly recommend PRC plots as the most informative visual analysis tool" for imbalanced binary classification, with precision (PPV) reported directly and compared against the prevalence baseline rather than the ROC diagonal — [same PLOS ONE source](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0118432)
- A 2024 counter-paper directly disputes this: it argues the "PR is better under imbalance" claim is a misinterpretation, and instead finds that ROC curves are robust to class imbalance while PR curves are highly sensitive to imbalance in ways that make cross-dataset/cross-condition PR-AUC comparisons unreliable — [The ROC curve accurately assesses imbalanced datasets, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11240176/)
- Balanced accuracy = (sensitivity + specificity) / 2 = macro-averaged recall; it equals plain accuracy exactly when classes are balanced, and its main value is penalizing a trivial majority-class classifier that would otherwise score deceptively high plain accuracy; one survey separately warns that under extreme imbalance, balanced accuracy can itself overestimate performance on the minority class — [Absolute Evaluation Measures for Machine Learning: A Survey, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.03392)
- Single-seed deep-learning results are "one sample from the distribution of outcomes" the training procedure can produce; a breast-ultrasound segmentation paper on small datasets recommends at least 5 independent random seeds with mean±SD reported for every primary metric, and notes small datasets are exactly where seed variance bites hardest — [Specificity- and Calibration-Aware Breast Ultrasound Segmentation, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.22308)
- The DeepOBS optimizer benchmark states the general standard: run multiple seeds, report mean and standard deviation, to judge whether a performance difference between two methods is statistically meaningful rather than seed noise — [DeepOBS: A Deep Learning Optimizer Benchmark Suite, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.05499); seed-induced variance has been shown large enough to flip which of two methods looks better, with one cited study finding ~1.82% accuracy swings from seed choice alone — [search summary citing CIFAR-10-scale seed-selection study, via WebSearch]
- Colas et al. propose choosing the number of random seeds using statistical-power reasoning (t-test/bootstrap based), and separately warn against reporting only the best-performing run among several seeds — [How Many Random Seeds? Statistical Power Analysis in Deep RL, arXiv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.08295)
- Bootstrap confidence intervals for AUC become unreliable when the positive-class sample is very small (≤6) or contains ties — [arXiv 0905.3928](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0905.3928); for nested/clustered data (observations grouped within a larger unit — directly analogous to many timesteps nested within one trial), the standard row-level bootstrap understates uncertainty, and the fix is to resample whole clusters rather than individual rows — [MetricGate: cluster-correlated bootstrap guidance](https://metricgate.com/docs/roc-cluster-correlated-bootstrap/) (vendor/secondary source — flagged as lower-authority below)
- For strongly autocorrelated time series generally, the usual confidence intervals become too narrow because the effective sample size is overestimated; the standard correction (Bartlett-style, or the lag-1 approximation n_eff = n(1−r)/(1+r)) adjusts for this, though one study found the lag-1 formula itself gives unreliable results in short/real-world series, and recommends a floor of n_eff ≥ (number of independent sequences/units) plus a cluster-resampling cross-check — [A Framework for Initial Transient Detection in CFD, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.22618); [The Autocorrelation Blind Spot, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.14414)

### Inferences
- For this project, pooling all frames from all ~1900-step trials into one giant set and computing a single frame-level AUROC/PR-AUC/F1 treats every frame as an independent sample. Given the cited autocorrelation findings, the true effective sample size for that pooled metric is far closer to the number of TRIALS than the number of frames — so any confidence interval computed naively on pooled frames (e.g., via standard bootstrap resampling of rows) will be dramatically overconfident. This directly matters for comparing Model A vs. Model B, since their F1 scores (0.72 vs. 0.715) are close enough that spurious precision in the uncertainty estimate could make a non-difference look significant.
- The correct protocol synthesizing the cluster-bootstrap and trial-holdout precedents above: compute ONE scalar metric per trial (e.g., per-trial AUROC, or per-trial best-F1 at a fixed threshold), then report the mean and spread (SD, or a bootstrap CI resampled over trials, not frames) of that per-trial metric across trials. This both respects the "resample clusters, not rows" guidance for nested data and the "hold out the whole trial" precedent from the robotics-adjacent leakage literature.
- Given the live disagreement between Saito & Rehmsmeier and the 2024 counter-paper on whether ROC is "robust" or "misleading" under imbalance, the defensible choice for this project is to report BOTH AUROC and PR-AUC side by side, always alongside the explicit positive-class prevalence (base rate) as a number on the same page/figure — this sidesteps picking a side in an active metrics-literature dispute while still giving a report reviewer (e.g., an ISEF judge) the information needed to interpret either number correctly. Balanced accuracy is a reasonable simple secondary number to include since it is cheap to compute and intuitively interpretable for a judge audience, but per the survey caveat it should not be the sole imbalance-aware metric reported.
- A concrete, low-cost audit protocol combining seed-variance and trial-clustering guidance, feasible for a part-time high-schooler's compute budget: pick a small number of non-overlapping trial-level splits (e.g., 3–5 random group splits by trial, not full k-fold if compute is tight), and within each split run every compared model (pull-force-only baseline, raw-signal-only baseline, Model A, Model B, and any held-out-friction/held-out-object ablation) with ~5 random seeds, reporting mean±SD of the per-trial-aggregated AUROC/PR-AUC/balanced-accuracy. This is a synthesis across sources (none of them specify this exact combined recipe) sized to roughly 15–25 total training runs per model variant, which should be tractable even at the project's limited compute scale, but the exact number of seeds/splits that's "enough" versus "wasted compute" was not addressed by any source for a dataset this small and should be treated as a judgment call.
- For the specific controlled baselines named in the assignment (pull-force-only model, raw-signal-only model, friction-coefficient ablation under held-out friction/object conditions), the PR-AUC-with-explicit-prevalence practice is especially important: if held-out-friction/object test splits happen to have different slip prevalence than the training distribution (plausible, since different friction/object conditions could produic different slip rates), PR-AUC values across these ablation conditions are not directly comparable per Saito & Rehmsmeier's own baseline formula (PR-AUC baseline = prevalence) — the student should report per-condition prevalence alongside each PR-AUC so a reviewer can tell whether an apparent PR-AUC drop reflects a genuinely worse model or just a harder (more imbalanced) test condition.
