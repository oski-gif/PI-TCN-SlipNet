# Multi-contact grasp stability theory for a physics-informed slip feature/loss

## KQ1: What is grasp wrench space (GWS) analysis and how would it apply to this specific setup (5 point/soft contacts, each with its own friction coefficient and normal/tangential reading, resisting one known external pull force)?

### Takeaway
GWS analysis asks a *system-level* question — "can the convex hull of wrenches the whole contact set can jointly produce contain/resist the disturbance?" — rather than a *per-contact* question like "is finger i individually inside its own cone." For this project's setup (5 contacts, one dominant known disturbance = pull_force), the theoretically correct move is to stop scoring each fingertip in isolation and instead build a grasp matrix G that maps all 5 contacts' force intensities to one net object wrench, because that is exactly the object that load-sharing/redistribution operates on — a finger at its friction limit is not necessarily a problem if the matrix shows the other four can pick up the slack.

### Cited Findings
- The Grasp Wrench Space is formally defined as the convex hull of all wrenches that the contact set can impose on the object, built by approximating each contact's friction cone as a polyhedral cone (a finite nonnegative combination of boundary/edge forces) and mapping each edge force to a 6D (or 3D planar) elementary wrench `w_{i,j} = [f_{i,j}; (c_i − o) × f_{i,j}]`, then taking the convex hull (Minkowski-sum form `W_L∞` or union form `W_L1`) of all contacts' edge wrenches — [Multi-Fingered Robotic Grasping: A Primer, arXiv:1607.06620, Sec. 2.5.2, Eqs. 4 & 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.06620).
- A grasp achieves force closure if and only if the origin of wrench space lies strictly inside this convex hull; necessary (but not sufficient) is that the grasp matrix G have full row rank (rank 6 in 3D) — [arXiv:1607.06620, Sec. 2.5.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.06620).
- Grasp quality (Ferrari-Canny, written Q∞ / Q1 depending on which norm approximates the cone) is the distance from the origin to the *nearest facet* of that hull — larger distance = more margin before the grasp fails in its worst-case direction — [arXiv:1607.06620, Sec. 2.5.3, Eqs. 5 & 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.06620).
- A companion/duplicate definition from a broader survey: the GWS is "the convex hull of all possible wrenches that could be imposed through the contact points," and the grasp-quality value built on it (there called the "Largest-minimum Resisted Wrench," LRW) is "the minimum distance between the origin and the boundary of the GWS" — [Robotic Grasping from Classical to Modern: A Survey, arXiv:2202.03631, Sec. 3.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.03631). The same survey notes variants that use L1/L∞ norms, that decouple force and torque units to avoid an arbitrary balancing factor, and that use GWS *volume* instead of a distance so the metric is not reference-frame dependent — [arXiv:2202.03631, Sec. 3.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.03631).
- The grasp matrix G formalism (contact force/moment intensities → net object wrench, `w = G·l`) with each contact contributing 3–4 columns depending on contact type (frictionless point, frictional point, soft finger with torsion) traces to Kerr & Roth, "Analysis of Multifingered Hands," Int. J. Robotics Research 4(4):3-17, 1986 — cited secondhand via [arXiv:1607.06620, Sec. 2.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.06620); I could not locate an open-access copy of the original 1986 paper itself (closest accessible artifact is a same-year Berkeley ERL report, [ERL-86-43](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/1986/ERL-86-43.pdf), which I did not verify is the identical work).
- Kerr & Roth's key structural insight (reported secondhand, via search-engine synthesis of citing papers, not a primary-source quote): contact force intensities decompose into a *particular* component that produces the required net wrench on the object, plus an *internal-force* component that lies in the null space of G — i.e., changes squeeze/grip tightness without changing the net wrench at all. This is exactly the mechanism by which real hands redistribute load across fingers while holding an object still. No single clean URL confirms this beyond secondary descriptions, so treat as moderately-confident, not primary-sourced.
- The underlying contact friction model throughout this literature is the Coulomb cone `sqrt(f1² + f2²) ≤ μ·f_n`, `f_n ≥ 0`, with half-angle `arctan(μ)`; standard practice approximates this circular cone as a k-faceted pyramid (more facets = more accurate, more compute) — [arXiv:1607.06620, Sec. 2.3 & 2.5.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.06620).

### Inferences
- The project's nine failed per-fingertip formulations (ratio, min-aggregated force difference, sum-aggregated force difference, trend variants) are all, in GWS terms, attempts to query the *boundary condition at a single contact* rather than the *shape of the joint hull*. GWS theory predicts exactly the failure mode the project found diagnostically: individual contact margins can look saturated/noisy/flat while the grasp as a whole is nowhere near its true capacity, because the other fingers are absorbing load — min/sum collapse this redistribution structure away instead of modeling it.
- Because the project already knows the external disturbance is overwhelmingly one thing — `pull_force`, ramping steadily (per the project's own diagnostic finding that tactile sums are flat while pull_force rises cleanly before slip) — the full worst-case-over-all-directions GWS question (which needs a convex hull) is more than this problem needs. The *task-specific* question "can the current grasp configuration resist the one wrench we know is coming, right now, given how load is currently shared?" is a much simpler, directional, linear-algebra question (see KQ2) rather than a full hull/epsilon-quality computation.
- To build any version of G for this rig, the feature/loss computation (not necessarily the TCN's input features) needs each contact's position and normal direction relative to the object or palm frame, not just the scalar normal/tangential magnitudes currently read in. In MuJoCo this is available directly from `data.contact` at simulation time even though it is excluded from the leakage-safe 22-feature TCN input set — using it only inside a training-time physics loss (never as a model input) is consistent with how `pull_force` itself is already used as a plain input and not a "label."

- I did not confirm whether the project's MuJoCo setup currently logs per-contact position/lever-arm data (needed to build G); this is an implementation question for the student/report-writer to check against the existing sim logging code, not something resolvable by web research.

## KQ2: What's the simplest version of this theory that still represents a genuine upgrade over naive per-fingertip sum/min/max, implementable by a high schooler in available PyTorch/numpy?

### Takeaway
Skip full Ferrari-Canny (needs a combinatorial convex-hull computation that is not cleanly differentiable) and skip the per-contact ratio/difference features already shown to fail; instead implement the classical **grasp-matrix force-redistribution / internal-force decomposition** (Kerr & Roth's idea, simplified to a linear least-squares solve) restricted to the one known disturbance direction (`pull_force`) — this is a handful of lines of `numpy`/`torch` linear algebra per timestep, is a genuine theoretical upgrade because it lets fingers compensate for each other, and has direct precedent in recent differentiable-grasp-optimization papers that linearize the same cone constraints for exactly this reason.

### Cited Findings
- Standard grasp-force optimization poses the problem as: given the grasp matrix G (from contact positions/normals) and the external wrench to resist, find contact forces `f` (one normal + tangential pair per contact) satisfying `G·f = -w_ext`, minimizing some cost (force norm, or residual wrench), subject to each contact's force lying in its friction cone. Recent real-time formulations solve exactly this at every control step as a convex QP: minimize squared residual wrench subject to a linearized friction pyramid, a minimum-normal-force floor (to avoid the trivial zero-force solution), and actuator limits — [Real-Time Force Regulation for Whole-Hand Dexterous Grasping, arXiv:2609.30082](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.30082).
- The same QP-over-friction-cones structure (minimize/solve for contact forces balancing an external wrench like gravity, subject to per-contact Coulomb cones) appears in [GraspADMM, arXiv:2603.13832](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.13832), with an ADMM solver proposed as a lighter-weight alternative to generic QP/SOCP solvers.
- Because exact circular friction cones make the force-optimization problem a nonlinear/second-order-cone program, multiple papers approximate the cone as a pyramid with a small fixed number of facets (BODex uses 8-vertex pyramids) specifically to turn a quadratically-constrained QP into a linear-constrained QP that is fast and easy to differentiate through — [BODex: Scalable and Efficient Robotic Dexterous Grasp Synthesis, arXiv:2412.16490](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.16490). A comparison paper notes the same quadratic-cone-vs-pyramid tradeoff for grasp planning (quadratic cones are more SDP-efficient, polyhedral cones are more tractable for direct optimization) — [Deep Differentiable Grasp Planner for High-DOF Grippers, arXiv:2002.01530](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.01530).
- A differentiable force-closure estimator exists that avoids the usual nested/iterative optimization and can be evaluated in milliseconds, framed as an energy-based formulation — [Liu et al., arXiv:2104.09194](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.09194) — showing that fast, gradient-friendly surrogates for this exact class of problem are an active, workable research direction, not just a textbook abstraction.
- A fully closed-form, almost-everywhere-differentiable alternative to convex-hull distance exists in the literature (attributed to Zhu & Wang) — a signed distance function `d_Q` such that force closure holds iff a particular signed value is negative — reported secondhand as "closed-form and differentiable almost everywhere for suitable Q" — [per arXiv:1607.06620, Sec. 3.1.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.06620); I was not able to retrieve the exact formula from a primary source, only this secondary description, so it is noted here as a promising direction rather than something to implement directly from these notes.
- A cheaper, non-hull surrogate with a direct formula is available from Li & Sastry: the smallest singular value δ of the grasp matrix G. It requires only an SVD of G (no convex hull, no cone discretization), but it captures only *kinematic* conditioning of the contact geometry, not friction-limited force capacity, and the original authors themselves note it is not invariant to the choice of torque reference point — [Z. Li and S. Sastry, "Task-Oriented Optimal Grasping by Multifingered Robot Hands," IEEE J. Robotics and Automation 4(1):32-44, 1988; reported via arXiv:1607.06620, Sec. 3.1.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.06620); an open-access near-equivalent is the 1986 Berkeley ERL report ([ERL-88-66](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/1988/ERL-88-66.pdf)), not directly verified against the published journal version.
- Partial-hull acceleration ("Partial Quick Hull" — compute only the single hull facet nearest the origin instead of the whole hull) is reported as a way to speed up exact Ferrari-Canny-style computation without changing the answer — [arXiv:1607.06620, Sec. 2.5.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.06620) — relevant if the student wants an exact offline/evaluation-time metric rather than a training-time differentiable loss.

### Inferences
- Combining the findings above, the concretely implementable recipe I'd propose (genuinely new relative to the nine failed formulations, because it operates on the *joint* system rather than per-contact, and is linear-algebra-only so it needs no QP solver and is natively differentiable in PyTorch):
  1. At each timestep, build the grasp matrix columns from known contact geometry: for each of the 5 fingertips, its unit contact normal `n_i` and lever arm `r_i` (contact position relative to the object/palm frame) — both obtainable from the MuJoCo sim at training time. Each contact contributes a 3xN (planar, force-only) or 6xN (spatial, force+torque) block to G exactly as in the primer's grasp-matrix construction.
  2. Compute the particular (minimum-norm) force distribution that would exactly resist the known external wrench, via a linear least-squares / pseudoinverse solve: `f_particular = G^+ @ (-w_ext)`, using `torch.linalg.lstsq` (fully differentiable, no iterative solver) — this is the Kerr & Roth "particular solution" idea reduced to one line.
  3. Compare `f_particular`'s per-contact normal/tangential components against each finger's *actual, currently-measured* normal/tangential forces and its own `mu_i`: the feature/loss becomes something like `residual = ||f_particular_tangential_i| - mu_i * f_particular_normal_i||` summed or softmax-aggregated over fingers — i.e., "how far is the *theoretically required, load-shared* force distribution from violating someone's cone," rather than "how far is the *currently measured* raw per-finger force from violating its own cone" (the thing already tried and shown to fail).
  4. Simpler fallback if full G construction turns out to be impractical (e.g. contact-position logging isn't available): a load-weighted combination instead of min/sum — weight each finger's normalized margin `m_i = (mu_i*N_i − T_i) / (mu_i*N_i + eps)` by its share of total normal load `w_i = N_i / sum_j(N_j)` before combining (`Q ≈ Σ w_i · m_i`). This is not full GWS theory, but it directly encodes the one load-sharing idea GWS theory is built on (a lightly-loaded finger's margin shouldn't dominate the metric the way raw min does; a heavily-loaded finger's margin should dominate more than raw sum allows) and needs no new geometry data beyond what's already logged.
- Both the pyramidal-cone-linearization trick (BODex) and the "only need an SVD, no hull" trick (Li & Sastry δ) suggest that if full GWS/epsilon-quality still proves too slow or too non-differentiable once attempted, there is a clear fallback ladder: full Ferrari-Canny hull (most correct, least differentiable) → pyramid-linearized LCQP (good tradeoff, needs a differentiable QP layer like `cvxpylayers`/`qpth`) → least-squares/pseudoinverse grasp-matrix residual (fully differentiable, numpy/PyTorch only, no new dependency) → load-weighted margin combination (no new geometry needed at all, smallest step up from what's already been tried).

### Gaps
- I did not find a worked example or tutorial specifically aimed at a non-roboticist implementing a simplified GWS/grasp-matrix feature in PyTorch; the recipe above is my synthesis from the optimization-paper literature (which targets grasp *synthesis*, not online slip-feature engineering), not a direct citation of someone doing this exact thing.
- I could not obtain the exact closed-form of the Zhu & Wang differentiable signed-distance function, so I cannot say with confidence whether it is actually simpler to implement than the least-squares approach I'm recommending — it is flagged only as "reportedly differentiable almost everywhere," which is suggestive but unverified.
- Whether `torch.linalg.lstsq` or a pseudoinverse is numerically stable when G is near rank-deficient (e.g., fingers nearly collinear or redundant) is a numerical question the project would need to test empirically; grasp-theory papers simply note G can be singular/ill-conditioned (hence the δ metric) but I found no specific guidance for handling this in a loss function.

## KQ3: Are there other established grasp-quality metrics (e.g. epsilon quality, task-oriented quality) worth considering instead of or in addition?

### Takeaway
The single most relevant alternative for this project is **task-oriented grasp quality** (Li & Sastry, 1988) — because the project already knows the disturbance direction (`pull_force` dominates, per its own diagnostic finding), using a task-specific wrench/ellipsoid instead of the isotropic worst-case-over-all-directions epsilon-quality is a theoretically-justified simplification, not a shortcut; GWS *volume* metrics and the internal-force/squeeze framing are the other two concepts worth folding in.

### Cited Findings
- Task-oriented grasp quality (Li & Sastry, 1988) models the expected task disturbances as a "task wrench space" — originally a task ellipsoid in wrench space — and scores a grasp by how well it can resist wrenches confined to that region, rather than treating every direction in wrench space as equally likely — [reported via arXiv:1607.06620, Sec. 3.1.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.06620); original citation Z. Li, S. Sastry, IEEE J. Robotics and Automation 4(1):32-44 (1988).
- A 2021 formulation computes a task-dependent grasp metric directly as a second-order cone program (SOCP), avoiding the need to build the full isotropic GWS when only a specific task wrench distribution matters — [Computing a Task-Dependent Grasp Metric Using Second Order Cone Programs, arXiv:2104.12158](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.12158).
- A data-driven variant (Lin & Sun) replaces the uniform/ellipsoidal task wrench space with a non-parametric distribution fit from disturbances actually recorded during task demonstrations, scoring a grasp by how well it covers the empirically most frequent disturbances rather than a hand-picked ellipsoid — [Grasp Planning to Maximize Task Coverage, IJRR 2015, via USF](https://rpal.cse.usf.edu/products/ijrr2015b.pdf). This is conceptually close to what this project could do: fit the "task wrench" distribution from the actual recorded `pull_force` trajectories in the trial data rather than assuming a generic ellipsoid.
- A 2023 extension to task-oriented grasp synthesis proposes a differentiable estimator of the GWS *boundary* itself, plus a metric usable for tasks that are not pure force-closure (e.g., lid-screwing, button-pushing) — [Task-Oriented Dexterous Grasp Synthesis, arXiv:2309.13586](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.13586v1) — evidence that differentiable GWS-boundary estimation specifically for one known task direction is an active and tractable research target, reinforcing that the project's simplified single-direction version (KQ2) is reasonable rather than naive.
- GWS *volume* is used by multiple works as an alternative to a distance-to-boundary metric specifically because volume does not depend on an arbitrary choice of torque reference point/frame, which the distance-based Ferrari-Canny metric does — [arXiv:2202.03631, Sec. 3.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.03631), citing multiple prior works without a single clean independent URL for each.
- A recent (2026) preprint, FIRMGrasp, proposes a *friction-uncertainty-adjusted* version of the Ferrari-Canny epsilon metric and reports that, across 1,599 sampled grasps on two different hands, 53% of grasps that the nominal (fixed-mu) epsilon metric certifies as force-closure actually lose force closure once a plausible friction-coefficient tail/uncertainty is considered; their adjusted metric reportedly predicts shake/pick success better than the nominal metric, which they describe as performing near chance on a shake-success task — [FIRMGrasp: A Friction-Informed Risk Margin for Robust Grasp Synthesis, arXiv:2607.25049](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.25049). This is a single preprint I did not cross-check against peer review.
- A classic review paper exists specifically comparing many grasp-quality measures side-by-side ("Grasp quality measures: review and performance") — [UPC/PMC, PMC4457357](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4457357/) — but I only confirmed its existence and scope from search snippets and did not fetch/verify its specific content or conclusions; the report writer should treat its findings as unconfirmed until read directly if they are to be cited specifically.
- Minimal Work, a grasp-quality metric proposed specifically for deformable/hollow objects, is noted as an alternative to the standard rigid-body epsilon metric and GWS volume when object compliance matters — [Minimal Work: A Grasp Quality Metric for Deformable Hollow Objects, arXiv:1909.11226](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.11226) — potentially relevant if the grasped objects in the sim are not perfectly rigid, but I did not investigate this further since it was outside the core ask.

### Inferences
- Given the project's own diagnostic finding (pull_force rises cleanly and dominates; raw summed tactile signals are flat), task-oriented quality is a better theoretical fit than isotropic epsilon-quality for this specific failure mode — the "task wrench" here is not an unknown worst-case direction, it is a known, measured, time-varying scalar-ish quantity (pull direction is fixed by the trial setup), which is exactly the condition under which task-oriented metrics are supposed to outperform isotropic ones.
- The Lin & Sun "fit the task wrench distribution from recorded data" idea is directly transplantable: rather than hand-designing a task ellipsoid, the project could empirically characterize the distribution of `pull_force` vectors at slip onset across its hundreds of logged trials, and use that empirical direction (not an assumed isotropic worst case) as the one direction the least-squares redistribution check in KQ2 needs to resist.
- The FIRMGrasp friction-uncertainty finding is a useful independent piece of corroborating evidence for a different part of the project's thesis: even a conceptually correct nominal metric (fixed, trial-level mu_combined scalar — i.e., Model B) can fail to predict real outcomes once friction uncertainty/variability is in play, which is consistent with the project's own observation that adding a single mu_combined scalar barely moved F1. It suggests friction uncertainty (not just friction magnitude) may itself be worth exposing as a feature in later iterations, though this is speculative extrapolation on my part, not something FIRMGrasp itself tested in this project's setup.

- I found no evidence (positive or negative) specifically testing task-oriented quality metrics in a learned/neural-network slip-prediction pipeline (as opposed to classical grasp-synthesis planning) — the transplant from grasp-synthesis theory to a per-timestep learned feature/loss for an existing TCN is my inference, not a cited precedent.
- FIRMGrasp (arXiv:2607.25049) is a single very recent preprint; I could not find independent replication or peer-reviewed follow-up, so its specific 53% figure should be cited as preliminary, not established consensus.
