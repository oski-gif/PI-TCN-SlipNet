# Audit pull-force dominance before rebuilding physics

The realistically ambitious plan for this project is not a tenth per-fingertip physics-feature variant — it is to spend the first two to three weeks validating, with controlled experiments, the diagnostic that already broke nine experiments (pull-force dominance), and then relocate the Newton's-second-law machinery out of static feature engineering entirely, into the two efforts already decided: on-policy fine-tuning (idea 2) and a friction-cone projection layer (idea 3), evaluated as closed-loop control failures rather than frame-level accuracy. The research is unambiguous on one point: a residual computed from MuJoCo's own logged trajectories is mechanically likely to be ≈0 in both slip and stable trials, because the solver already enforces **M·v̇ + c = τ + Jᵀ·f** at every step ([MuJoCo docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/computation/index.html)) — so a new static Newton's-law feature would almost certainly reproduce the same null result the nine Coulomb-ratio variants already produced, just with a cleaner explanation of why. The genuine upgrade the grasp-stability literature offers is a *load-redistribution* feature, built from the grasp matrix rather than per-fingertip ratios, but even that should be tested only after a cheap methodology audit — label-horizon sweep, controlled baselines, trial-level statistics — because that audit could change which model is even worth improving. Idea 2 and idea 3 both have real precedent: a 2023 contact-implicit MPC paper took a near-identical label-free, on-policy residual-learning loop from 0% to ~90% task success on hardware ([arXiv 2310.09893](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.09893)), and a closed-form three-case friction-cone projection formula exists that a high schooler can implement in a few lines of `torch.where` ([arXiv 2101.11763](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2101.11763)). Scoped week-by-week from mid-October to March with explicit go/no-go gates, this plan fits a part-time high schooler's calendar, and — critically for an ISEF submission under deadline pressure — it has a legitimate, citable fallback even if every ambitious piece stalls: the rigorous negative-results story itself (pull-force dominance, demonstrated and quantified; 9+ physics formulations, ruled out with controlled, properly-powered experiments) is a defensible scientific contribution on its own.

## Two audits should run before a single new feature is coded

Everything downstream of the current baseline is cheaper to get right first than to redo later, and the horizon/metrics research makes a specific, actionable case for why. The project's diagnostic plot — pull_force rising cleanly over a ~180-timestep pre-slip window while summed tactile signals stay flat — is exactly the shape that the early-warning literature treats as a *tunable horizon problem*, not a fixed one: a vineyard-disease study that swept 1–3, 3–7, and 5–10 day prediction windows found the middle window won on F1 across all four model families tested, and explicitly found that narrower horizons hurt by shrinking the positive class ([arXiv 2605.04548](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.04548)). No source gives a closed-form rule for picking horizon H in a slip-prediction task — the single most directly relevant precedent, a BioTac slip-prediction horizon study, exists but its horizon-ablation table could not be extracted from the PDF in this research pass and is worth the student opening by hand ([Veiga et al., ToH 2018](https://www.ias.informatik.tu-darmstadt.de/uploads/Publications/veigaToH2018.pdf)) — so the right move is an empirical sweep (H ∈ roughly {10, 30, 60, 100, 150} timesteps), reporting F1, PR-AUC, and resulting positive-class prevalence at every H, before touching model architecture again. The labeling rule itself has a standard, citable form directly transferable here: label timestep t positive if the event occurs anywhere in [t, t+H], negative only if the window is confirmed stable for the *entire* span, and drop — don't guess — timesteps too close to a trial's end to evaluate unambiguously ([critical-care early-warning benchmark, arXiv 2411.16346](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.16346)); what that source does not resolve, and what this project has to decide for itself, is how to treat frames *after* slip has already begun, since the literature's framing assumes early-warning tasks that don't revisit post-onset frames.

The second audit — controlled baselines — is where the pull-force-dominance hypothesis gets either confirmed or falsified in a way an ISEF judge can trust. A pull-force-only model and a raw-tactile-only model, trained and evaluated identically to Model A and Model B, directly test whether the dominant signal really is the ramping pull force; if the pull-force-only baseline alone nearly matches Model A/B's F1 of ~0.72, that is a strong, citable confirmation of the project's own diagnostic rather than an assumption. Running this honestly requires getting the statistics right, and here the literature has a live, unresolved fight the report should not pretend is settled: Saito & Rehmsmeier's influential 2015 result shows ROC curves can hide a real precision collapse under class imbalance — identical ROC points can correspond to 75% precision in one setting and 25% in another — and argue PR curves are the more informative tool ([PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0118432)), while a 2024 counter-paper argues the opposite, that ROC is actually the imbalance-robust metric and PR-AUC is the one that is unreliable across conditions with different prevalence ([PMC11240176](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11240176/)). The defensible move given that dispute is to report both AUROC and PR-AUC side by side, always alongside the explicit positive-class prevalence, since PR-AUC's own random-chance baseline *is* the prevalence rather than 0.5 — not to pick a side in an active methods argument inside a high-school science fair writeup. Underneath both metrics sits a second statistical trap specific to this data: trials are ~1900 timesteps of strongly autocorrelated frames, so pooling all frames and bootstrapping rows treats far more "samples" as independent than actually exist, understating uncertainty — the fix, consistent with both the cluster-bootstrap literature for nested data and this project's own trial-level train/test splitting, is to compute one scalar metric per trial and resample whole *trials*, never frames, for any confidence interval or seed-variance estimate. A tractable protocol for a part-time student's compute budget is 3–5 trial-level group splits × roughly 5 seeds per model variant (~15–25 runs per variant), reporting mean±SD of per-trial-aggregated AUROC/PR-AUC/balanced accuracy — a synthesis across sources rather than one paper's named recipe, so it should be treated as a reasonable judgment call, not an established standard.

## The physics term needs to move, not multiply

The grasp-stability research gives a real reason the nine failed formulations failed, and it is not "the math was wrong" — it is that every one of them asked a per-contact question (is finger *i* individually inside its own friction cone) in a setting where the theoretically correct question is systemic: can the *whole* five-contact grasp, as currently load-shared, resist the one disturbance everyone already knows is coming ([grasp wrench space primer, arXiv 1607.06620](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.06620))? Classical grasp theory (Kerr & Roth, reported secondhand via citing papers since the 1986 original was not independently located) decomposes contact forces into a component that balances the external load and an internal component that redistributes squeeze across fingers without changing the net wrench at all — which means a finger sitting at its own friction limit is not necessarily a problem if the other four are absorbing the slack, and min/sum aggregation (what the project already tried) collapses exactly that redistribution structure away. The directly implementable upgrade is a grasp-matrix load-redistribution feature: build G from each fingertip's contact normal and lever arm, solve `f_particular = G⁺ @ (-w_ext)` via a one-line differentiable least-squares pseudoinverse for the load distribution that *would* resist the known pull force, and compare that theoretically-required distribution's per-finger margins against the raw measured ones — a genuine step up from the failed formulations because it is linear-algebra-only, needs no QP solver, and models load-sharing instead of discarding it. Because the project already knows the disturbance direction (pull_force dominates and ramps cleanly), this also lines up with task-oriented grasp quality (Li & Sastry, 1988, reported via the same primer), which scores a grasp against a known task-specific wrench rather than an isotropic worst case — a theoretically-justified simplification here, not a shortcut. If full grasp-matrix construction proves impractical, there is a documented fallback ladder ending in a load-weighted margin, `Q ≈ Σ wᵢ·mᵢ` with wᵢ the finger's share of total normal load, which needs no new contact-geometry data beyond what is already logged.

That fallback matters because a direct check of this project's own simulation code shows the full version is not currently buildable without new infrastructure: `closed_loop_sim.py`'s `read_features()` and `generate_trials.py`'s per-trial logging record touch/normal/tangential/torque_mag per fingertip plus object position, velocity, and pull force — not each fingertip's contact position or normal direction relative to the object frame, which building G actually requires. That is a real, non-trivial addition (new sensor/geometry logging in the MuJoCo scene, not a training-script change), and should be scoped as such rather than assumed free.

The more consequential reframing, though, concerns the Newton's-second-law idea that was deferred months ago over "force-accounting ambiguity." That ambiguity turns out to be a non-issue in simulation specifically — MuJoCo exposes ground-truth per-contact force directly via the documented `mj_contactForce` function, already resolved into normal/tangential/torsional components in the contact frame ([MuJoCo API reference](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/APIreference/APIfunctions.html)) — so object-level force accounting (`m·a = m·g + F_pull + ΣF_contact`) is bookkeeping, not new science. But that same transparency is why a *static* Newton's-law feature or loss computed on logged trajectories is the wrong move: because MuJoCo's forward-dynamics solver computes every trajectory by enforcing the equation of motion at each step, a residual checked against that same ground truth should be expected to come out near zero in both slip and stable trials — mechanistically the same flat, non-discriminative signature the project already diagnosed in summed normal/tangential force and torque_mag. This is the researcher's own inference from the documented equations rather than a result found explicitly stated anywhere, and it is cheap enough to verify directly — compute r(t) on a handful of already-logged trials before committing further design time — but it is consistent with how every actual precedent uses a physics residual: PIPER's analytic-dynamics regularizer penalizes how far a *policy's chosen action* would violate the equations of motion, not how far a solver's own already-self-consistent output deviates from them ([arXiv 2603.14469](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14469)), and the broader physics-informed-ML literature evaluates residuals on a *network's candidate solution*, never on labeled data that already satisfies the physics by construction ([arXiv 2006.08472](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.08472)). The right home for this machinery is therefore not a new standalone feature at all — it is exactly the residual used inside the already-decided on-policy fine-tuning loop (idea 2), where genuine model-versus-physics mismatch, not solver self-consistency, is what gets penalized.

## Idea 2 and idea 3 both have real precedent and a concrete home in the existing harness

The on-policy fine-tuning plan is not speculative; it matches a published, hardware-validated pattern closely enough to treat as direct precedent rather than an analogy. A 2023 adaptive contact-implicit MPC paper from the Posa lab learns a residual on top of a physics-based contact model using *only* observed closed-loop state transitions — no ground-truth labels — via a QP-based loss comparing the prior model's predicted next state against what was actually observed, and reports the controller going from 0% to roughly 90% success at initiating a fruit-rolling task after adaptation, converging within about 10 trials on a hardware pushing task ([arXiv 2310.09893](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.09893)). That is structurally this project's idea 2: train baseline, run closed loop, collect visited states, fine-tune on a physics-residual loss alone, repeat — and the research argues the residual should be the whole-hand Newton's-law balance (m·g, F_pull already a model input, ΣF_contact,i via `mj_contactForce`, object acceleration from simulator state), not a reintroduced per-fingertip ratio, since the aggregate framing is what the Posa-lab precedent actually uses and the per-fingertip framing is what already failed nine times. LIFT, a 2026 framework pairing deterministic on-policy data collection with exploration confined to a learned world model, is a second structural match worth noting — but its architecture and ablation claims here are drawn only from secondary summaries (a blog aggregator and a non-English third-party review), since the primary PDF could not be parsed in this research pass, and should not be cited as settled fact about LIFT's internals without the student independently checking the paper's HTML version ([arXiv 2601.21363](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.21363)). One concrete implementation caution from an adjacent paper: staging physics-only fine-tuning before labeled fine-tuning (or vice versa) in the wrong order has been shown to make a model's sub-modules mismatch and degrade performance ([arXiv 2412.09116](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.09116)), so the fine-tuning order should be a deliberate, tested choice rather than an assumption.

Idea 3's friction-cone projection layer is the more mechanically concrete of the two. A closed-form, three-case piecewise formula projects any `(normal, tangential)` force pair onto the Coulomb cone with no iterative solver: if the point is already inside the cone it passes through unchanged, if it is on the "wrong side" entirely it collapses to the apex, and otherwise it lands on the boundary via a closed-form expression ([arXiv 2101.11763](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2101.11763)) — directly implementable with `torch.where`, independently per fingertip, with no coupling across contacts needed at this project's scale. The real pitfall is not the forward pass but the backward one: two independent 2026 papers report that hard projection layers can produce rank-deficient, ill-behaved Jacobians right at the constraint boundary that stall gradient flow into the upstream network ([arXiv 2602.03461](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.03461); [arXiv 2605.11526](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.11526)), which argues for instrumenting gradient norms at the projection boundary during training and keeping a smoothed/regularized cone (shown in one optimal-control paper to stay differentiable everywhere while still reproducing exact sticking behavior) as a documented fallback if gradients misbehave. One source paper's own boundary-case term did not pass an independent algebraic sanity check against the cone's definition, so it should be re-derived from `‖tangential‖ ≤ μ·normal` directly rather than copied verbatim — a concrete implementation risk, not a hypothetical one. Favor this closed-form, hand-derived route over general differentiable-convex-optimization libraries: `cvxpylayers`, the standard tool for exactly this class of projection, has reportedly gone unmaintained since around 2024, a real compatibility risk under a hard March deadline ([Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/how-to-encode-constraints-to-the-output-of-neural-networks-9bce302b9687)).

Both ideas plug into `closed_loop_sim.py` without new infrastructure: `predict_slip()` already has the model-swapping interface, and `decide_action()` is the one integration point explicitly left as a stub. The concrete experiment is to keep the pull-force ramp generation and `decide_action()` fixed, swap in no-physics, soft-physics-loss, and hard-projection model variants behind `predict_slip()`, and log outcome per trial rather than a single pass/fail bit. The published closed-loop literature converges on a reusable three-way failure taxonomy worth adopting wholesale: missed-slip/object-drop, false-alarm/unnecessary-regrasp, and — critically, and easy to miss — control-induced failure, where the corrective action itself causes the loss (SlipSense attributes 5 of 100 failures specifically to the prevention motion pushing the object out, not to missed detection — [arXiv 2609.15910](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.15910); a center-of-mass grasp-planning paper likewise counts false slip predictions as failures in their own right, not just misses — [arXiv 2006.00906](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.00906)). None of the published closed-loop papers runs this exact three-way comparison or gives a standardized trial count — reported counts range from 10 to 100 trials and look pragmatically hardware-time-limited rather than power-analysis-driven — so roughly 100 trials per variant (matching the SlipSense scale, easily affordable in pure simulation) with a deliberately-included low-friction/near-failure regime is a reasonable, citable-as-precedent-informed but ultimately self-justified choice, not a number taken from any single paper.

## What is genuinely too ambitious, and the fallback that still counts

Several pieces of this plan are more fragile than they look on paper and should be named as such rather than discovered under deadline pressure. Full multi-contact grasp-matrix construction is the single biggest risk: it requires per-contact lever-arm and normal-direction logging that, confirmed directly against this project's own `generate_trials.py` and `closed_loop_sim.py`, does not currently exist — only scalar touch/normal/tangential/torque_mag per fingertip and object-level position are logged — so this is new simulation infrastructure, not a training-script change, and the load-weighted-margin fallback (needing no new geometry) should be treated as the default, not the backup plan. The ROC-AUC-versus-PR-AUC question is a live, unresolved dispute in the metrics literature itself ([PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0118432) vs. [PMC11240176](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11240176/)) — the report should present both metrics with explicit prevalence and should not claim either one is "the" correct choice, since doing so would mean taking a side an ISEF judge's own reviewer could reasonably contest. Several specific numbers in the supporting research carry real provenance caveats that must not be laundered into settled fact: LIFT's internal architecture is known only from secondary/non-primary summaries; FIRMGrasp's reported 53% figure comes from a single very recent, not-yet-independently-replicated preprint; Kerr & Roth's foundational internal-force/null-space mechanism is relayed only through later papers citing it, not the original 1986 text; and the Zhu & Wang closed-form differentiable force-closure formula could not be retrieved from a primary source at all. None of these should be cited in the ISEF writeup with more confidence than the notes themselves carry.

Given those risks, the fallback is not a consolation prize — it is a legitimate, independently defensible ISEF contribution on its own terms, and the plan should be written and executed so that it survives even if idea 2 and idea 3 both underperform. Rigorously demonstrating pull-force dominance — not as an offhand diagnostic plot but as a controlled-baseline result with trial-level statistics, both AUROC and PR-AUC, explicit prevalence, and a clean pull-force-only model that nearly matches two more elaborate architectures — is itself a real finding about this class of slip-prediction problem. Paired with a disciplined accounting of why nine (and potentially more, once the load-redistribution feature is tested) physics-informed formulations failed, including the mechanistic argument for *why* a logged-trajectory Newton's-law residual should be expected to be uninformative in a solver-consistent simulation, this is a negative-results narrative with genuine methodological teeth: it correctly diagnoses a failure mode (per-contact framing discarding load-redistribution, and physics checks run on already-physics-consistent data) rather than just reporting that something didn't work. That framing should be built into the project from Phase 0 onward, not assembled after the fact as a rescue if March arrives early.

## Conclusion

The project's real pivot is not "try a different physics feature" — it is recognizing that the right physics question for this grasp is systemic (can the whole hand, as currently load-shared, resist the one known disturbance) rather than per-contact, and that the right *place* for exact force accounting is inside a feedback loop that visits genuinely physics-violating states (on-policy fine-tuning), not as a static check against data a constraint solver already made self-consistent. That reframing turns two previously separate threads — the deferred Newton's-law idea and the already-decided idea 2 — into the same piece of work, which is a meaningful simplification of scope, not an addition to it. The second underappreciated point is procedural: because the horizon and baseline audits are both cheap and capable of changing which model variant is even worth carrying into idea 2 and idea 3, doing them first is not just methodological hygiene, it is the single highest-leverage use of the student's limited weekly hours between now and March — and it is also exactly the kind of rigor an ISEF judge can verify and credit, regardless of which contribution tier the project ultimately lands on.
